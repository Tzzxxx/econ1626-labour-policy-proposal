# Tracking Generative AI’s Effects on Australian Jobs: A Monitoring Proposal for Jobs and Skills Australia

**Prepared for:** Jobs and Skills Australia

**Policy context:** This proposal recommends adding a two-year AI and workforce monitoring pilot to Jobs and Skills Australia’s 2026–27 work program.

## Executive Summary

Jobs and Skills Australia (JSA) has published a national study on generative AI and the Australian labour market. It finds that generative AI is more likely to augment jobs than replace them, but effects vary across occupations, industries, regions and worker groups (JSA 2025). This study provides a useful baseline, but ongoing evidence is needed to track changes in vacancies, tasks, wages, job quality and training needs. 
This proposal recommends a two-year AI and Work Monitoring Pilot within JSA’s 2026–27 labour-market and future-work programs(JSA 2026). The pilot would update an occupation-level dashboard annually and combine existing labour-market, vacancy and training data with a small employer and worker survey. It would report results by region and relevant worker groups. After comparing three options—no additional monitoring, recurring JSA monitoring, and direct training subsidies—the proposal recommends the pilot. It would strengthen future policy decisions while staying within JSA’s evidence and advice role.

## Problem Definition
Generative AI may automate some tasks while helping workers complete others. JSA’s national study finds that augmentation is currently more likely than job replacement, but the effects vary across occupations, industries, regions and worker groups (JSA 2025). AI exposure measures indicate which tasks could be affected; they do not show by themselves whether employment or job quality will actually change. Realised effects also depend on how widely employers adopt AI and how workplaces reorganise tasks (JSA 2025).

The policy problem is therefore uncertainty about how AI adoption is changing Australian jobs over time. JSA’s study provides an important baseline, but policymakers need regular evidence on employment, vacancies, wages, job quality, worker transitions and training needs. Without consistent monitoring, it may be difficult to distinguish AI-related changes from broader economic trends or identify which occupations and groups may need support. This proposal addresses that evidence gap through a two-year monitoring pilot.
## Labour Market Impacts

### Employment and Tasks
Generative AI changes tasks within occupations; exposure alone does not forecast job loss. It may automate routine tasks while complementing analysis, communication and decision-making. Three mechanisms shape labour demand: displacement, as capital performs tasks previously done by workers; productivity gains, which may lower costs and expand output; and new task creation, which can increase labour demand (Acemoglu & Restrepo 2019). The balance depends on adoption, workplace redesign, output growth and worker mobility (JSA 2025). These effects may occur at different speeds: adoption can be rapid while workers need time to retrain or move, creating short-term adjustment costs. JSA’s exposure measures indicate potential task-level effects, not actual adoption or employment outcomes (JSA 2025). The pilot should compare employment and vacancy trends across higher- and lower-exposure occupations, using ABS data as a macroeconomic benchmark rather than treating aggregate changes as evidence of an AI effect (ABS 2026).
### Wages and Distribution
Workers whose skills complement AI may benefit from productivity gains and increased demand, while workers in substituted tasks may face weaker demand or retraining needs. Wage effects also depend on how firms share productivity gains. OECD’s multi-country worker and employer evidence identifies possible gains, including wages and job satisfaction, alongside risks such as work intensity and bias (OECD 2023). ILO estimates show women have a higher share than men in its highest exposure group. This is potential exposure, not evidence of actual job loss or wage decline, and it is not Australia-specific (Gmyrek et al. 2025). Monitoring should disaggregate wages and training access by occupation, gender, age and region.
### Industry Structure
JSA reports faster adoption among larger firms and market-sector organisations (JSA 2025). If firms with better access to data, skills and investment adopt earlier, productivity gains may be uneven, widening performance gaps. Adoption may also change occupational mixes and demand for workers who integrate AI into existing processes. The pilot should track adoption and workforce outcomes by industry and firm size to identify emerging skill needs and adjustment pressures.
## Policy Options and Evaluation
The options are evaluated for the quality of evidence, equity, feasibility, and indicative two-year cost.

Option 1-Continuing current monitoring. JSA uses its own AI study, dashboard, and labour market data without any additional program budget ($0 incremental cost). It is feasible and cheap, but it will not reliably demonstrate whether AI exposure leads to changes in employment, wages, or skills over time (JSA 2025).

Option 2–Two-year Pilot of AI and Work Monitoring (recommended).JSA matches the employment and vacancy statistics from ABS to AI exposure and adoption statistics, produces an annual update of an occupation dashboard, conducts surveys among employers and employees in selected industries. The indicative cost of Option 2 is $1.0 million: $560,000 for two APS6 analysts for two years (with 30% on-costs) and an assumed cost of $440,000 for surveys, data matching, and dashboard development. The estimate is based on the Australian Public Service Commission's indicative average base salary of APS6 for 2025 (APSC 2025). Option 2 has the highest evidence and equity value and is moderately complex to implement.

Option 3–Targeted training vouchers.The government provides 1,000 workers in selected high-exposure occupations with vouchers worth $2,000 each, plus the assumed cost of $200,000 for the administration process ($2.2 million over two years). It provides a concrete service but targets the wrong occupations if the assumption about the relationship between exposure and job losses is wrong, and does not measure wider task and industry effects.

For evidence and targeting, Option 2 is better; the status quo is cheaper and easier, while vouchers provide direct assistance but are potentially less targeted.
## Recommended Package and Implementation Plan
JSA should implement Option 2: a two-year AI and Work Monitoring Pilot. This fits JSA’s role in providing evidence and advice on Australia’s workforce and skills needs, and aligns with the 2026–27 work program’s focus on labour-market evidence (JSA 2026). The indicative budget is $1.0 million over two years, based on the staffing, survey and dashboard assumptions set out above.

Months 1–3—Design. A JSA project lead should establish a technical team and stakeholder reference group, including ABS, employers, workers, unions and training providers. The team should define indicators, select occupations and industries using JSA’s exposure and adoption data, and set privacy and survey protocols.

Months 4–9—Baseline. JSA should combine published ABS employment and vacancy data with its AI exposure and adoption measures and a small employer and worker survey. The baseline dashboard should compare higher- and lower-exposure occupations and report outcomes by industry and demographic group where data quality permits.

Months 10–24—Updates and evaluation. JSA should publish an annual update, compare occupation-level changes while accounting for wider labour-market trends, and release a final evaluation by month 24. It should advise government on whether the evidence supports targeted training or adjustment measures.

Key performance indicators: baseline dashboard published by month 9; first annual update by month 12; coverage of at least six occupation groups and three industries; and final public report by month 24. Success should be judged by timely reporting and useful, disaggregated evidence, rather than promising employment gains the monitoring pilot cannot directly cause.
## Risks and Mitigations
Three risks require active management. First, employment and wage changes may reflect economic conditions as well as AI. JSA should compare higher- and lower-exposure occupations, account for industry trends, and describe results as associations rather than proof of causation.

Second, exposure is not the same as adoption or job loss, while small survey groups create privacy and representativeness risks. JSA should publish methods and uncertainty, combine exposure measures with adoption and outcome data, de-identify responses, and suppress small cells (JSA 2025; OECD 2023).

Third, low employer or worker response could delay or skew findings. A stakeholder reference group, accessible survey formats and use of existing ABS data can reduce this risk. Review participation and data coverage at month 12; adjust the survey before the second annual update if targets are missed.
